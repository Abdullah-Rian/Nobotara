Nobotara71 — 30-second demo video

NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Challenge: Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars · Audience: school-age kids · Version 2

The idea in one line: Left on the Moon. Still flying. Still waiting.

The challenge asks for stories of hardware that was left behind, including probes "still traveling away from Earth". Our three missions cover all three kinds: Apollo 11's lander left on the Moon, Voyager 1 still flying out of the solar system, and Perseverance's sample tubes still waiting on Mars. The video is built around that, in the order the team agreed: team, challenge, our approach and why it works, the missions, the AI guide, then the outcome and hope.

1. What the research says

The rules (2025 Project Submission Guide; the 2026 guide comes out on 13 November)

  • Every team must submit a project demo. A video demo must be 30 seconds or shorter.
  • Host it on YouTube or Google Drive with a public link. It cannot be uploaded to the Space Apps site.
  • English subtitles are required if the video is in another language. Burn them in anyway: judges often watch with the sound off.
  • The guide suggests opening with who you are and your team name, and asks for strong visuals and readable text.
  • Submissions have a "Use of Artificial Intelligence" section. The 2025 storytelling winner listed every AI tool it used.

Judges' own advice from Space Apps: give presentation as much attention as the other four criteria, because a solution can't make a difference if people can't see why it matters.

What the challenge text asks for (and our answer)

  • "tell the story of … equipment that introduces school-age space enthusiasts to the hardware and the science it made possible" → three playable missions for kids.
  • "descent stages", "rovers", "probes still traveling away from Earth" → Eagle's descent stage, Perseverance, Voyager 1.
  • "showing your audience how these missions operated rather than listing facts" → kids drive, steer and solve, they don't read lists. This is our "why it works".
  • "Accuracy is important throughout" → narrated facts checked against NASA sources; the AI guide answers from NASA pages.

What past winners did

  • 2025 Best Use of Storytelling, "A Solar Tale" (HerCode Space): the Sun tells its own story to children. Science, history and imagination in one narrative, with all its references from NASA and NOAA, and every AI tool declared.
  • 2025 Art & Technology, Zumorroda-X: a pixel-style learning game built on NASA data. A game can win when it teaches clearly.
  • The pattern: one clear story, the product shown working, the "why it matters" said in plain words, and an emotional last line.

The winners' videos are on YouTube, which asked for a sign-in in our test browser, so this is based on their project pages and the official guidance, not a frame-by-frame study. Watch two or three of them before recording: past winners are listed at spaceappschallenge.org/2025/meet-the-2025-global-winners/.

2. The challenge · 0:03.5–0:10

Voice-over (0:03.8–0:09.4): "NASA left machines on the Moon, Mars and beyond. Few kids know their stories."

Visual: Quiet, slow shots of the hardware sitting alone: Eagle's descent stage on the grey Moon, Voyager 1 small against the dark, Perseverance's tubes at Jezero. Then the challenge title.

Why: This is the problem the challenge describes: kids mostly hear about active missions, not the machines left behind. Saying it in the video shows judges you answered this challenge (Relevance).

3. Our approach · 0:10–0:14.5

Voice-over (0:10.3–0:14.3): "So we made them playable. Kids remember what they do."

Visual: Fast cuts of a kid's hands at work, one per word on screen:

  • DRIVE: the rover rolling up to Eagle on a phone held sideways;
  • EXPLORE: tapping a label ("Eagle Descent Stage") and the short card opening;
  • SOLVE: the "Put the plan in order" puzzle locking in green.

Why: This is the argument that convinces. The challenge asks to show how missions worked instead of listing facts, and doing beats reading for kids. The shots prove it is real and works on a phone.

6. Production rules

  • Length: 30 s or less, including the end card.
  • Captions: burn in English captions for every line, at the bottom, large. Put the titles (the on-screen text above) at the top so they don't collide.
  • Voice: warm, curious, talking to a 10-year-old, not a trailer voice. If the narration is AI, list the tool under "Use of Artificial Intelligence". Say too that the game's narration is AI-generated (Gemini text-to-speech).
  • No NASA logo (the meatball or the worm). Its use is restricted and it can suggest endorsement. Credit NASA sources in text instead.
  • Music: royalty-free, soft at the start, rising at part 4, quiet under the last line. Credit it in the submission.
  • Export: 1920 × 1080, 30 fps, H.264 MP4. Upload unlisted or public, and check the link works when signed out.

7. Before recording, decide

  • Team names and roles: Arijit or Argho Talukder; Samin as Project Director or Lead Researcher.
  • Whether to say where the team is from: a country name in part 1 costs about 1 s.
  • Which future missions to name on the "NEXT" card, if any.
  • Who narrates: a team member's own voice usually feels more genuine to judges than an AI voice.
  • Re-read the 2026 Project Submission Guide when it is published (13 November) and adjust if the rules changed.
